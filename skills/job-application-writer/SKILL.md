# Job Application Writer

This skill writes two pieces for a job application: a cover letter, and a resume tailored to one posting (a summary line plus bullets). It works from what the user gives you: their resume, the posting, their own account of the work, and any writing of theirs. The aim is a piece that sounds like the person who sends it and stays true.

This folder works on its own. If the slop-scrub skill is installed beside it, run slop-scrub on the result as the last step.

## Rule zero: use only what the user gave you

This rule sits above every other rule here. When another rule would need a fact you don't have, this rule wins and the piece gets shorter.

- Every fact in the piece comes from the user: each number, employer, title, date, skill, tool, team size, result and name. Take them from their resume, their notes or their messages to you.
- Facts about the company and the role come from the posting, and the piece presents them as the posting's.
- Never invent or inflate. Keep every number exact, so 40% never becomes "over 40%". Keep the size of their verb: "helped" never becomes "led", and "worked on" never becomes "owned". Never merge two bullets into one bigger claim. Never narrow a date: a job that ran from 2021 to 2023 doesn't tell you anything happened "in 2022".
- Never add a skill or a tool that the posting asks for and the resume doesn't show.
- Never invent a thing that happened, and never grow a bullet into one. No scene, no teammate, no weekday, no "what broke first" and no "what I found", unless the user told you. A bullet that says check-in went from 3 minutes to under 1 doesn't tell you what the desk was like before.
- Never invent a voice. Add no feeling, opinion, joke or reason for applying that the user didn't give you, so no "I'm excited", "I'm passionate" or "I'd be a great fit".
- Never hand the user someone else's part. A story with the user nowhere in it isn't theirs to tell yet, so ask what their part was (Step 3). In every story, each act stays with the person who did it.
- When two things the user gave you disagree, use neither and ask. A resume that says "led" beside notes that say the manager ran it is one case, and 40% beside "a third" is another. "Make me sound more senior" adds no fact, so change only which true things lead.
- When the piece needs something the user didn't supply, leave it out and list it under "What I still need from you" (see Hand over).

If you have no resume and no notes, you have nothing true to write from. Ask for the resume and stop there.

## What decides how a letter reads

Read this before you plan a letter, because it decides what you build on.

- A letter built on **happenings**, the things the user lived and told you about, reads as a person wrote it.
- A letter built on resume bullets reads as machine-written however you word it. A bullet is a **claim**: a result summed up afterward. Writing it as a first-person sentence doesn't turn it into a happening.

Tests in November 2026 on one of the leading AI writing detectors split on that line ("What this rests on" has the counts). No wording, order, length, tone or voice sample moved a letter made of claims. Made-up detail didn't move one either, and rule zero forbids it anyway. So your first job is to find the user's happenings, and your second is to keep their telling and keep the claims few.

## Words this skill uses

- **Happening:** a thing the user took part in and told you about in their own words. It took place once, and it holds what a bullet leaves out: what they saw, who was there, what they did first, what came back. "One evening I checked the drafts by hand, and two of them came back flagged" is a happening.
- **Claim:** a result summed up afterward, which is what a resume bullet is. "At Northwind I merged three billing spreadsheets into one" is a claim, whether it sits in a bullet or in a sentence.
- **Account:** the user's own telling of their work, typed fast, dictated, or already sitting in their notes and messages.
- **Always-true sentence:** a sentence with no time in it, true on any day. It can be a principle, a promise, how you'd work, or interest in the role. "I care about clean handoffs" is always-true.
- **Ration:** the cap on claims and always-true sentences together. After the first line, they take at most one word in five of a letter built on an account.
- **Tie line:** a short sentence that says a happening is the task the posting names. It is a claim about the user, so rule zero binds it.
- **Mark:** a habit from the user's writing that a reader would know them by, such as a bracketed aside or a short verdict.

## Step 1: Sort what you were given

1. **Their own words about this work.** Look in every place they've already told it: the request itself, pasted notes, a voice memo transcript, earlier messages, and any file of their stories they pointed you to. A letter is built on these (Step 3).
2. **Resume.** The source of facts and claims.
3. **Posting.** The source of the role's name, the company's name and what the job needs. It never supplies a fact about the user.
4. **Writing samples,** such as posts, emails or an old letter of theirs. A sample may supply voice. It never supplies structure, and it never stands in for happenings (Step 5).

Then name your case twice:

- **Material:** you hold an **account** (at least one happening in their words) or you hold **bullets only**.
- **Voice:** **Case A, a sample given** (any finished writing of theirs, even three short posts on another subject) or **Case B, no sample.**

## Step 2: Ask for the account first

This step is for cover letters, and a resume request gets written in this reply (Step 6).

- **The message already holds an account** (things they lived, in their own words): write the letter now.
- **It holds only a resume and a posting:** the first reply is the ask below, and no letter.
- **They say "write it now", say they can't answer, or asked for the piece alone:** write the short bullets-only letter from Step 4, and say in one line what it is unless they asked for the piece alone (Hand over).

The ask takes two minutes to answer. Send it in these words, and leave out the last question if they already said why:

> Before I write, tell me about one or two lines on your resume. Two minutes, typed fast or spoken, and rough is good.
>
> - What was going on that day or that week, and who was there?
> - What was your own part: what did you do first, and what did you see or find?
> - What happened next, with the numbers?
> - Why do you want this job?
>
> A letter built on your own telling can say what only you know. Say "write it now" and I'll write a short letter from the resume alone.

When they answer, build the letter on it (Step 3). One happening is enough to write from, and you may ask for one more. If an account tells what other people did and never says the user's part, ask "what was your part?" before you use it.

## Step 3: Find the happenings

**With an account, build on it.**

1. Pick one to four happenings that answer the posting's most important needs. Use a happening only when the user is in it or told you their part. Otherwise ask. A top need with no happening and no bullet behind it stays out of the letter and goes in Hand over, and no story gets stretched to cover it.
2. Keep their words: their names for things, their details, their numbers and the order things went in. You may cut, reorder, fix spelling and split a long run where it already joins. You may also change the tense, or retell a happening plainly with every fact kept, so the letter doesn't read like pasted notes. Keep the turns of phrase only they would write, and never trade their words for grander ones. Call other people by their role, never by name, unless the user asks.
3. Tell it with a full subject: "I rebuilt", never "Rebuilt". Say who did what as they told it. Where they said "we" or "the staff", keep it, and never move another person's act to the user.
4. Join the act to its result with "and" or "so", never with an "-ing" tail such as ", cutting errors by 30%".
5. Keep the time as vague as they kept it. "One evening in November" stays as it is and never becomes a date.
6. Add only a supplied fact or "and". Use "so" or "because" only where the user said one thing caused the other. If a happening needs a fact they left out, ask for it or leave the gap.
7. Leave the other happenings and the other bullets out, because the resume goes with the letter.

**With bullets only, you have claims and no happenings.** Don't dress them up. In the tests a claim retold as a before-and-after story, put in formal words, kept in the user's own bullet wording or given a voice read the same as the plain claim. Use these rules:

1. Pick two or three bullets that answer the posting's top needs. If one of the posting's top needs has no bullet behind it, leave it out of the letter and list it in Hand over.
2. From a bullet you may use the action, the thing acted on, its number, the result the bullet states, and the employer, title and dates of that job. Nothing else.
3. Tell it only as far as the bullet goes: "I moved our billing onto a new payment processor, and failed payments fell by 40%". The rules above on subjects, joins and time apply here too.
4. Keep the user's plain words and their trade words. Swap a buzzword for plain speech only when the bullet says what it means, and otherwise keep their word.
5. A present-tense duty such as "Manage a team of 6" goes in as a clause, or stays out.
6. Two bullets from the same job may share a sentence when they name the same project or system. Never link two bullets the resume doesn't link.

## Step 4: Write the cover letter

Write the plain letter first in both voice cases, and in case A put the voice on afterward (Step 5).

**Shape**

- **Greeting:** "Dear [name]," when the posting or the user gives a name. Otherwise "Dear [Company] hiring team,".
- **First line:** one sentence of 20 words or fewer, in the first person, that says what the letter is: "I'm applying for the Billing Operations Lead role at Contoso." It may also give their title and employer from the resume. Add a reason only if the user gave you one.
- **Body:** one to four paragraphs, one happening each. Each paragraph opens on its happening, and happenings take at least two words in three of the body. The body ends on a happening or its tie line.
- **Claims:** the few plain facts from the resume that the posting asks about, such as years, a team size or a second language. They go in one sentence right after the first line, in the same paragraph. A claim that belongs to one happening may follow that whole happening as one sentence. Never set a claim between two sentences of one happening or after the last happening, and leave the rest to the resume.
- **Tie lines:** one per happening at most, and most happenings get none. A tie line is a claim about the user, so rule zero binds it, the size of the verb included. Write one only when all three of these hold: the user did the task themselves, it is the very task the posting names at the size the posting names it, and their own words in the account or the resume say so. Helping to plan a trip isn't leading one, and recommending one volunteer isn't recruiting. When any of the three fails, write no tie line. The story stands on its own, and the gap goes in Hand over. A line such as "That's the same cleanup the posting lists first." has to pass that test like any other, and each tie line gets words of its own, because a borrowed frame reads like a form. A tie line runs 15 words or fewer, sits right after the happening it points to and opens by pointing back at that story. Never open it on the posting's own words ("Your posting asks for..."). Never set two always-true sentences back to back, and that includes across a paragraph break.
- **Close:** at most one closing sentence of 15 words or fewer, with no fact and no feeling in it, in a paragraph of its own: "Please let me know if you'd like to talk." Never two closing sentences, such as a thank-you line followed by a request to talk.
- **Sign-off:** the user's name.

**The bullets-only letter.** Write this one only when Step 2 sends you here. Keep the greeting, the first line, the close and the sign-off from the shape above. The body is two or three short paragraphs. Each one takes one need from the posting and opens on the claim that answers it, with its number when the bullet has one. A second claim from the same job may follow, and so may one tie line under the rule above. Lead with what the resume shows. A gap the user mentioned goes in only when the posting sets a condition and the resume meets it, such as a degree in progress, and then as a plain fact. Keep the letter under about 150 words.

**Length comes from happenings.** The letter is as long as its happenings make it, and it stays under about 250 words. Never pad it out to a usual letter length, because padding can only come from claims, always-true sentences or invented detail. If the letter is short, tell the user it's short on purpose and that one more happening makes room for more.

**Sentences**

1. Keep the ration. After the first line, claims and always-true words together stay at one in five or under, and about 15 in 100 is the target. A bullets-only letter can't meet this, so there it applies to always-true words alone.
2. Keep the average sentence at 17 words or fewer, with none over 33. Split a long packed sentence where it already joins, and that goes for a long sentence of the user's too.
3. Don't open two paragraphs in a row the same way. If one opens on a place ("At Northwind I moved..."), open the next on the thing ("The vendor sign-up form I rebuilt...") or on the act ("I trained six support agents...").
4. Summing-up numbers (a percent, "3x", "40+", "8 of 10", "up to", "every", "across") stay at two per 100 words or fewer, and one per paragraph. Counts, money and step counts are fine.
5. Speak to the reader as "you" where a person would, and keep most sentences about the work.
6. Cut transition openers such as "Early on,", "Next,", "In that role," and "Additionally,", along with "I also" chains and "-ing" openers such as "Using data, I...".
7. Don't open on a span of years, as in "Over eight years..." or "From 2019 to 2023 at...".
8. Don't polish a plain happening into a tidy formula such as "When X happened, I did Y and saw Z". Tell it the way they told it.
9. A habit in the present tense ("I check the logs first") is always-true. Retell it as a happening ("I checked the logs first") only when the user told you about a time they did it, and otherwise leave it out.
10. Call each thing by one name every time: the company, the product, the role.
11. Cut stock letter lines: "I am writing to express my interest", "proven track record", "results-driven", "hit the ground running", "I believe I would be a great fit" and "thank you for your time and consideration".
12. Use contractions where they sound natural: I'm, I'd, didn't, it's. Inside the user's own sentences, keep the ones they used.
13. Use no em dashes and no en dashes.

## Step 5: Voice

**Never treat a writing sample as a template.** Never copy its outline, its order or number of paragraphs, its rhythm or its stories, and never pour the user's facts into its sentences. This holds when the sample is an old cover letter of theirs too. Its facts are theirs to use and its voice may carry. Its feelings and its reasons for applying stay out, and the new letter gets its own plan from Step 4.

**A sample is not an account.** Three posts on another subject tell you how the user writes and nothing about the work, and in the tests a voice laid on a bullets-only letter didn't change how it read. An account in their words already carries their voice, so with an account you need few marks.

**What a sample may supply:** their words for things, how they join sentences, whether they contract, their punctuation habits and how they open.

**What it may never supply:** the outline, the paragraph rhythm, the content (facts, stories, or opinions on other subjects), or any sentence of theirs.

**Case B, no sample.** Write the plain letter and stop there. Use plain words and natural contractions, and add no jokes, asides or feelings the user didn't give you. With bullets only, tell the user the letter is in no one's voice yet.

**Case A, a sample given.**

1. Write the plain letter first (Step 4), without looking at the shape of the sample.
2. Study the sample in eight lines or fewer, each with a quoted example of ten words or fewer: what they call things, how they join sentences, whether they contract, what they do with numbers, how they open, and any aside habit.
3. Keep only the habits that fit a letter to a hiring manager: everyday words, contractions, joins, a bracketed aside that is a true side note, or number shorthand that keeps the value exact. Leave out hashtags, slang, chat shorthand such as "btw", all caps, emoji, dares and stacked exclamation marks.
4. Lay the voice on in this order:
   - their words for things, swapped into the sentences you wrote, never into theirs;
   - their joins and contractions, inside those sentences;
   - their way of opening, on the first line, only when it still says what the letter is and stays at 30 words or fewer. In one test a plain first line read better than one in the user's voice, so when in doubt keep it plain;
   - how long their sentences run and how they join, each written fresh.
5. Carry three marks when three fit the letter and fewer when fewer fit, each one used once. Never put two in one sentence, and keep at least one full sentence between any two.
6. Prefer marks that live inside a sentence, such as a join or an opening word. A mark that is a sentence of its own sits after a whole happening, and never between the first line and the first happening, never inside a happening and never last.
7. Never set a short mark of 12 words or fewer next to another short sentence. Short sentences in a row read like a list of verdicts, so join one of them to its neighbor with "and".
8. Copy no sentence and no run of four or more words from the sample. A short verdict goes in only when their account gives one about this work.
9. Carry a stance (an opinion of theirs) only when the sample shows their view on this kind of work. Otherwise carry none, and never write a generic opinion to fill the gap.
10. Voice changes wording only. It never adds a fact, a number or an event, and it never makes a happening bigger.
11. Tell the user which habits you carried and which you left out, because only they can say it sounds like them.

## Step 6: Tailor the resume

**Be plain with the user about what this part does.** Tailoring here means choosing and ordering the user's own bullets, and it keeps their wording. In the tests, resumes that people had written read as a person's writing, and resumes an AI had written did not. Two of the people's resumes were tailored five ways by choosing, reordering, cutting and rewording bullets, and all five still read as a person's. One full rewrite, with a new layout and a new summary, did not. So keep their wording and their layout. Never bend a resume toward a score.

**Work in this order, and stop as early as the posting allows.**

1. **Choose.** Keep the bullets that answer the posting and drop the ones that don't. Choosing and cutting must never make the resume say more than the user said. A line that says how long, how much or at what level they did something stays in whenever the posting sets a bar on that same thing, even when it shows them short of the bar.
2. **Reorder.** Under each job, put the bullets that match the posting's top needs first.
3. **Cut.** Trim a long bullet to the part the posting cares about, in the user's words.
4. **Reword last, and as little as you can.** Change a word only where the posting's word names the same thing the bullet already describes, and never bring in a named tool, method or title. Leave every other bullet as the user typed it, though you may fix a spelling slip.
5. **Summary line.** Write one or two sentences, 35 words or fewer in all, built from phrases already on their resume: their title, what their dates plainly show, and two things from their bullets that the posting asks for. Count years only when the dates make the count plain, and tell the user how you counted. Add no adjective they didn't give you, such as "seasoned" or "results-driven".

**Never change** a number, an employer, a title, a date, a degree or the size of a verb. Never add a skill, a tool or a keyword the resume doesn't support.

**When a bullet has to be written fresh,** because the user asked for it or a long bullet has to split in two:

1. Open on a past-tense verb, as resumes do, though the present tense is fine for the duties of a current job.
2. Give each bullet one act, with its number kept exact.
3. Join the act to its result with "and", or put the result after a semicolon. Use "so" only where their bullet says one caused the other.
4. Keep each bullet to 25 words or fewer, and never stack three results in one.

**Source map.** After the resume, list each bullet you changed beside the bullet or bullets it came from, so the user can check every word.

**Gaps.** List each thing the posting asks for that no bullet supports, and each bar the user's own lines fall short of, and ask the user about each one. Never fill a gap yourself, and never hide one by dropping the line that shows it.

## Hand over

Give the user these, in this order. If they asked for the piece alone, send only the piece, and leave out of it whatever you would have had to explain:

1. The piece.
2. What you used. For a letter, say which part of their account or which bullet each paragraph came from. For a resume, give the source map.
3. With a bullets-only letter, one plain line saying what it is: true, short and built from the resume alone. Say the ask from Step 2 still stands.
4. What I still need from you: each missing fact as a short question, such as why they want this job or a number a bullet left out.
5. In case A, the habits you carried and the ones you left out.

## Self-check before you hand over

Run each check on the finished piece and fix what fails.

1. Every number, employer, title, date, skill and tool in the piece appears in what the user gave you, and every fact about the company appears in the posting.
2. Each verb and each date is the same size the user gave it, no two bullets became one claim, and where two things they gave you disagreed you used neither.
3. Nothing happens in the piece that the user didn't tell you about, no bullet grew into a scene, and every story shows the user's own part and leaves each act with the person who did it.
4. The piece holds no feeling, opinion or reason for applying that the user didn't give you, no "so" or "because" they didn't give you, and no other person's name.
5. With an account: each body paragraph opens on its happening, happenings take two words in three of the body or more, and the claims sit right after the first line or after the happening they belong to.
6. After the first line, claims and always-true words stay inside the ration, and no two always-true sentences sit back to back. No claim sits between two sentences of one happening. Each tie line names a task the user did themselves, at the size the posting names it, in words they gave you.
7. No two paragraphs in a row open the same way.
8. The average sentence is 17 words or fewer, the longest is 33 or fewer, and the first line is 20 or fewer (30 with a voice opening).
9. No two short sentences in a row each make a claim with no join between them.
10. Summing-up numbers stay at two per 100 words or fewer, and one per paragraph.
11. The letter has at most one closing sentence, in a paragraph of its own, with no fact and no feeling in it.
12. In case A, there are three marks at most, each spaced by a full sentence. No sentence and no run of four or more words is copied from the sample, and nothing comes from its outline.
13. There are no em dashes and no en dashes.

**The checker script.** This folder holds `letter_check.py`, which tests the letter rules that can be checked by counting. It needs only Python 3, makes no network calls and writes no files.

1. Save the finished letter as plain text and run `python3 letter_check.py letter.txt --source resume.txt posting.txt`. Add each file of the user's notes after `--source` too.
2. In case A, add `--voice` when the first line uses their way of opening, so it may run to 30 words.
3. Fix each line marked `fail`, then run the script again. It exits with 0 when nothing fails.
4. Read each line marked `check this` against the user's files. A clean run never proves the facts are true.
5. The script ends with the rules it can't judge, such as the ration and the tie lines, so check those by reading.

If slop-scrub is installed beside this skill, run slop-scrub on the result too.

## What this rests on

The rules come from 93 texts scanned on one night in November 2026, on one of the leading AI writing detectors. The job seekers were made up, and the accounts were real people's.

- **From a resume and a posting alone:** 24 letters, written with the first version of this skill, with no skill and in a dozen other styles. None read as a person's writing. One had invented detail added as a check, and it read the same.
- **Built on a real person's account, by the rule writer:** 35 letters on six accounts, and 25 read as a person's writing. Six of the ten misses were every letter on one short account an AI had worded. The other four set resume claims after the last happening, set a claim between two sentences of one happening, set two unrelated resume results in a row, or opened in the user's voice with the close joined on.
- **Built on a real person's account, by fresh writers:** twelve letters by writers who saw only a request card and the version before this one, on eight accounts by seven authors. All twelve read as a person's writing. Four letters written from the same cards with no skill did not.
- **Resumes:** five that an AI had written read as machine-written. Three work histories that people had written read as a person's writing: two sample resumes a government agency published and one letter from 1859. The two sample resumes were tailored five ways, from reordering alone to rewording every bullet, and all five still read as a person's. One full rewrite did not.
- **This version before its last fix, by fresh writers:** 19 pieces by writers who saw only a request card and this text as it stood before the tie-line and choosing rules were tightened. On the same eight accounts, 5 of 6 letters with no writing sample read as a person's writing, and the sixth read as machine-written. With three short posts on another subject as the sample, all 6 letters did. All 7 resumes tailored from five sample resumes a government agency published did too. A fact check of all 19 found nothing made up, but two tie lines claimed a fit the request didn't back, and both copies of one resume dropped a line that showed the user short of the posting's bar. Those findings are why the tie-line and choosing rules read as they do now.
- **This version, by fresh writers:** 9 pieces on four of the same accounts and two of the same sample resumes. Of 3 letters with no writing sample, 2 read as a person's writing and the third didn't. All 3 letters with three short posts as the sample did, and so did all 3 tailored resumes. A fact check of all 9 found nothing made up, nothing made bigger and no fit claimed. No letter had a tie line, and both resumes on the request that had lost a line in the round before kept it word for word. Two letters said less about the job than their versions in the round before.

What this doesn't cover: one account told mostly as a scene, with little in it that the user did, passed 3 times and failed 2 times across the rounds, so an account that says little of the user's own part can go either way. Every account was polished, published writing, so no account typed fast by a user has been tried. No resume that a job seeker typed for themselves has been tried, and no hiring manager has judged a letter. Never promise the user a score, and never add typos, odd characters or broken grammar to change one.
